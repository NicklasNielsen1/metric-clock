La décade · this week of ten days

The seven-day week was abolished. Every tenth day, Décadi, was the day of rest. Every day was named after a plant, every fifth after an animal, every tenth after a farm tool.

    A short history

    In 1793 the young French Republic set out to remake time itself. Weights and lengths were going decimal, so why not hours? A decree split the day into 10 hours of 100 minutes of 100 seconds. Clockmakers built dials with two rings of numbers, old and new, but almost nobody wanted to throw away their watch. Decimal time became compulsory in 1794 and was dropped only about six months later, in April 1795.

    The calendar lasted much longer. Year I was counted from 22 September 1792, the day after the monarchy was abolished, which also happened to be the autumn equinox. The poet Fabre d'Églantine named the months after the weather of the French countryside: the misty month, the snowy month, the windy month. He gave every single day a plant, animal, mineral or tool, replacing the saints' days of the old calendar.

    The ten-day week was unpopular: one rest day in ten instead of one in seven. Napoleon brought back the Gregorian calendar from 1 January 1806. It came back once more, for a few weeks, during the Paris Commune of 1871, which dated its decrees in Year LXXIX.

    This page keeps both systems running. Decimal time here is counted from your own local midnight, so your noon is exactly 5 decimal hours. The year starts on the day of the autumn equinox at the Paris Observatory, which is the original rule; the equinox is calculated with astronomical formulas, good to within a few minutes.
